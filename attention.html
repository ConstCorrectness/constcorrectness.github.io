Why does Splitting Dimensions Create
Better Understanding?

In Transformer models like BERT or GPT, we often take a massive vector (e.g., 256 dimensions) and slice it into smaller chunks (e.g., 8 heads × 32 dimensions).

It seems counterintuitive: How can "dumbing down" the resolution from 256 to 32 help the model see more?

1

The "Blurry Generalist" Problem

A single large head tries to capture grammar, tone, references, and logic all in one dot product. The signals get muddled.

8

The "Team of Specialists" Solution

Splitting dimensions allows each "Head" to project the input into a unique subspace. One looks for pronouns, another for prepositions.

Let's look at the numbers. The total parameter count remains roughly the same because we divide the dimension size ($d_{model}$) by the number of heads ($h$). Adjust the sliders to see how the per-head dimension ($d_k$) changes.

The Mathematical Insight:
We aren't losing data. We are reshaping it. Instead of one long vector of size 256, we now have 8 independent vectors of size 32. Each head can now learn a different linear projection matrix ($W^Q_i, W^K_i, W^V_i$).

2. The "Context Subspace" Theory

Why does this help? Imagine a single attention head is a "General Manager". They try to oversee everything—grammar, facts, tone—but miss the details.

Multi-head attention creates a "Team of Specialists". By projecting the 256 dimensions down to 32, each head is forced to focus on a specific subspace of the language features.

Why divide by $\sqrt{d_k}$? When dimensions ($d_k$) are large (e.g., 256), the dot products can grow huge. This pushes the Softmax function into regions where gradients are extremely small (vanishing gradients). By splitting 256 into 8 heads of 32, we keep $d_k$ small (32). The dot products stay in a manageable range, making training more stable and allowing the model to distinguish subtle differences in meaning.
